How to practise IELTS speaking alone (and actually improve)

~4 min read

"Just find a speaking partner" is the most useless advice in IELTS prep — useless because most learners can't. Partners flake, tutors cost money, and language-exchange apps match you with someone at your level who can't correct you. The good news: solo practice, done right, beats casual partner practice. Here's how.

1. Record everything, listen once

Your phone is the strictest examiner you'll ever meet. Record a 2-minute answer, then listen back with a single question in mind: "Where did I hesitate?" You'll hear your own fillers, repetitions, and the exact moment your grammar wobbled — things no one notices while speaking. One listen per recording; the point is diagnosis, not self-torture. Delete after. The improvement compounds weekly.

2. The 60-second cue card sprint

Real Part 2 gives you one minute to prepare and two to speak. Most solo practicers skip the prep minute — fatal, because planning under pressure is itself the skill being tested. Drill it properly: set a timer for 60 seconds, jot three bullet points (no full sentences), then speak for the full two minutes without stopping. Do one card a day. Within two weeks the "running dry at 70 seconds" problem disappears.

3. Shadow native speakers

Pick a 2-minute clip — a podcast, a TED talk, a news report. Play a sentence, pause, repeat it mimicking the rhythm, stress and intonation exactly, like an echo. This is called shadowing, and it's the highest-ROI pronunciation drill in existence. Ten minutes a day rewires your word stress and sentence melody faster than any amount of "repeat after me" vocabulary work. Your accent doesn't need to change; your clarity will.

4. The paraphrase game

Lexical resource — 25% of your score — is largely the ability to say the same thing different ways. Drill it directly: take a simple sentence ("Pollution is a big problem in cities") and rephrase it five ways without repeating key words ("Urban areas struggle with severe contamination…", "Air quality in metropolitan centres has deteriorated…"). Do five sentences a day. When you blank on a word in the exam, this reflex saves you — and examiners explicitly reward it.

5. Get scored, not just practised

Here's the honest limitation of solo practice: you can't grade yourself. You don't know the band descriptors the way an examiner does, and "that felt okay" is not feedback. This is the gap AI fills. After any solo session, get an external score — on the four real criteria, with one concrete thing to fix. Otherwise you're rehearsing the same mistakes with increasing confidence.

Three traps of solo practice

Solo practice fails in predictable ways. Trap one: only practising Part 2, because cue cards feel like "real" practice — while Part 1 and 3, which reward quick thinking and opinion-building, get ignored. Trap two: never using a timer, so your two-minute answers are really four-minute rambles that teach you nothing about exam pacing. Trap three: practising at half voice — mumbling into your phone on the bus. Pronunciation and fluency only improve at full speaking volume, with your mouth actually doing the work. If a drill feels comfortable, it's probably not working.

The routine

Twenty minutes a day: one cue-card sprint (5 min), one shadowing session (10 min), five paraphrases (5 min). Once a week, do a full mock and get it scored. Solo practice done this way isn't a compromise — for most learners it's better than an unreliable partner, because every minute is deliberate.
