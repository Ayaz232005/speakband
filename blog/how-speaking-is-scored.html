How IELTS speaking is really scored

~5 min read

Most test-takers walk into the speaking test with a vague idea of what the examiner wants: "speak well, don't make mistakes." The reality is far more mechanical — and far more useful to know. Your performance is scored against four published criteria, each worth 25% of your speaking band. Understanding them is the closest thing to seeing the exam paper in advance.

1. Fluency and coherence (25%)

This is not "speaking fast". It's speaking at length without unnatural hesitation, and linking your ideas so the listener can follow. An examiner at band 6 hears frequent self-correction and repetition; at band 7 they hear occasional hesitation but a speaker who keeps going and connects ideas with markers like "however", "for example", "as a result". The single biggest lever here: don't stop. A fluent wrong turn beats a perfect silence.

2. Lexical resource (25%)

Vocabulary — but not the way most students think. Examiners don't count rare words; they reward precision, collocation (words that naturally go together), and paraphrase. Saying "exhausted" instead of "very tired" matters. So does being able to rephrase when you forget a word — that's a band 7 skill hiding in plain sight. Memorised phrases, by contrast, score zero: if it sounds recited, it doesn't count.

3. Grammatical range and accuracy (25%)

Range means variety: simple sentences mixed with complex ones — conditionals, relative clauses, perfect tenses. Accuracy means your errors don't block understanding. Here's the part students miss: at band 7, errors are explicitly allowed. The descriptor says "frequent error-free sentences" — not perfect sentences. A speaker who attempts complex grammar and slips occasionally outscores a speaker who plays it safe with simple sentences and makes no mistakes at all. Caution is penalised; ambition is rewarded.

4. Pronunciation (25%)

The most misunderstood criterion. It is not about accent — a strong accent with clear sounds scores fine. Examiners listen for: individual sounds (can you be understood?), word stress ("PHOtograph" vs "phoTOgraphy"), sentence stress and intonation. At band 6, mispronunciation sometimes forces the listener to concentrate; at band 7, it's "easy to follow throughout". Shadowing native speakers for ten minutes a day — repeating exactly what you hear, rhythm and all — is the fastest fix here.

What separates a 6 from a 7

Read the descriptors side by side and a pattern emerges. Band 6 is "willing but limited": willing to speak at length but hesitates, willing to use complex sentences but they break, vocabulary that's adequate but repetitive. Band 7 is "sustained and flexible": keeps going, self-corrects smoothly, paraphrases around gaps, mixes sentence types with control. The jump isn't about perfection — it's about recovery. Band 7 speakers make mistakes; they just don't let mistakes stop them.

How the four become one band

Your overall speaking band is the average of the four criteria, rounded to the nearest half band. A 7, 7, 6.5, 6.5 averages to 6.75, which rounds up to 7. A 6.5 across all four stays 6.5. This arithmetic matters more than most learners realise: it means your weakest criterion drags the average down twice — once in its own 25%, and again by capping what the other three can lift. The fastest route to a higher band is almost always fixing your single lowest criterion, not polishing your strongest. If you don't know which one is lowest, that's the first thing to find out.

How to use this

Stop practising "speaking" in general. Practise one criterion at a time: a week of discourse markers for fluency, a week of precise adjectives for lexis, a week of conditionals for grammar, a week of shadowing for pronunciation. Then test yourself against all four at once.
