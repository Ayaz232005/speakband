5 mistakes that keep IELTS speakers stuck at band 6

~4 min read

Band 6 is the most crowded score in IELTS speaking. It's where competent English goes to stall: the speaker is understandable, the grammar mostly works, and yet the score won't move. After looking at how examiners describe the 6-to-7 boundary, the same five habits show up again and again. Here's what they are — and the drill that fixes each one.

1. Answering Part 1 like it's a police interview

"Do you like music?" — "Yes." That's a band 6 answer to a band 7 question. Part 1 wants 2–3 sentences: answer, extend, give a reason or example. "Yes, I do — mostly while I'm commuting. There's something about old rock songs that makes traffic bearable." Same English level, completely different score. The examiner can't reward range you never show.

The drill: take ten Part 1 questions and force yourself to add one extra sentence to every answer. Time it: if your Part 1 answers average under 15 seconds, you're under-answering.

2. Memorised answers

Examiners are trained to spot them, and the penalty is brutal — a memorised stretch of speech gets you no credit for fluency, vocabulary, or grammar. The tell-tale signs: unnatural intonation, vocabulary far above the rest of your answer, and irrelevance to the actual question. A "perfect" memorised paragraph about your hometown scores worse than a hesitant honest one.

The drill: ban yourself from preparing full sentences. Prepare ideas only — three bullet points per cue card, in note form. If you catch yourself reciting, stop mid-sentence and restart in your own words. That recovery is itself a band 7 skill.

3. Fillers instead of fillers-that-work

Every speaker hesitates. Band 6 speakers fill the gaps with "uh", "um", long silences, or repetition of the question. Band 7 speakers hesitate too — but with language: "That's an interesting question…", "Let me think…", "Well, I'd say…". These are called discourse markers, and they're free points. They keep your speech flowing while your brain catches up, which is exactly what "fluency" means to an examiner.

The drill: record a 2-minute answer. Count your "uh"s. Then re-record the same answer with five discourse markers deliberately placed. Compare. The second version will sound a full band higher.

4. Safe vocabulary on repeat

"Very good", "very bad", "very important", "a lot of people think…" — band 6 speakers lean on a small set of all-purpose words. The examiner's lexical resource criterion explicitly rewards precision and collocation: not "very tired" but "exhausted", not "very important" but "crucial" or "essential". You don't need rare words; you need the right common ones.

The drill: take your last practice transcript and circle every "very + adjective". Replace each with a single precise word. Do this for a week and your lexical range visibly changes.

5. Never finishing Part 2 properly

The cue card gives you a minute to prepare and up to two minutes to speak. Band 6 speakers routinely run dry at 60–70 seconds and trail off with "…so, yeah." An examiner reads that as limited ability to speak at length — a direct hit on fluency. The fix is structural: one minute per bullet point on the card, plus a closing line. Four bullets, four minutes of material, and you'll finish the two minutes with room to spare.

The drill: do five cue cards this week with a strict structure: 20 seconds per bullet, then a one-sentence conclusion ("So overall, that's why this mattered to me."). Use a timer. Boring? Yes. Effective? Completely.

The pattern

Notice what none of these mistakes are about: grammar rules, accent, or "advanced" English. Band 6 to 7 is almost never about knowing more English — it's about showing the English you already have, for longer, with better structure. That's good news, because it's trainable in weeks, not years.
